# Calibrate against the corpus without averaging it

Use this reference for explicit voice recovery, follow-ups to prior posts, major rewrites, or a
final fidelity pass where the current conversation is not enough evidence.

Do not make corpus inspection a prerequisite for every first draft. The blog changes over time,
and delaying reaction surface in order to mine style can be counterproductive.

## Choose neighbors, not a random sample

Inspect a small set of posts that are relevant to the current piece. Prefer similarity in one
or more of these dimensions:

- same project or conceptual lineage;
- same language;
- similar function (confession, technical explanation, argument, fragment, music note, etc.);
- similar emotional temperature;
- explicit continuation/follow-up;
- a post Franklin identifies as a good or bad precedent.

Recent posts matter when the question is current voice. Older posts matter when they are the
actual conceptual parent. Do not assume recency always wins.

## Read for decisions, not statistics

Do not average sentence length, heading count, meme frequency, or reference density into a
house style.

Instead ask what decisions recur and why:

- What did the author choose to notice?
- Where did he explain and where did he trust the reader?
- Where did a tangent remain open?
- What kind of certainty did the prose permit?
- Which sentences carry unmistakable selection/judgment?
- What formal move solved a local problem rather than serving as branding?

The goal is to recover **decision patterns**, not surface frequencies.

## Use negative evidence

A corpus is useful for identifying tics too.

Check whether the proposed draft is repeating something merely because recent posts already
did it:

- same cryptic closer;
- same confession-then-joke rhythm;
- same named thinker;
- same meme/template;
- same “this reminds me of…” turn;
- same section shape;
- same rhetorical question;
- same explanatory aside.

A move repeated often can be a motif. It can also be model lock-in. Ask whether it still does
work in this post.

## Prefer Franklin's reactions over corpus inference

If Franklin says “this does not sound like me,” that is stronger evidence than ten old posts
suggesting the opposite. The corpus is historical evidence, not an authority over the living
author.

Likewise, if he deliberately wants a register unlike anything previously published, do not
normalize the draft back toward the corpus.

## Hrönir and other evaluations

When the blog repository contains reader-perspective evaluations, use them as evidence about
what particular posts achieved for particular readers — not as a universal scoring function.

A post may succeed because of weird clarity, operational usefulness, factual rigor, lyrical
integrity, comedy carrying argument, outsider accessibility, or another property. Do not make
every future post maximize the winners of previous comparisons.

Evaluations are especially useful for discovering that the corpus has **multiple successful
registers** and for identifying failure modes that repeat across them.

## Output of a calibration pass

Keep the result compact. Internally identify:

- the 2–4 closest corpus neighbors;
- what this draft should preserve from them, if anything;
- what it should deliberately not repeat;
- any clear continuity fact the reader needs;
- whether the current draft is converging toward an accidental average of the corpus.

Then revise the draft. Do not turn the calibration into visible literary criticism unless the
user asked for it.
